Calibrated Anchors

Company disclosures are biased in measurable, persistent ways. This system measures that bias from the frozen corpus and corrects for it, instead of asking a model what it thinks a number will be.

The idea in one paragraph

We are not forecasting the future. Every target period had already closed before the corpus was frozen on 14 August 2026 — Hays' financial year ended 30 June, ADI's third quarter around 1 August. The job is to reconstruct an already-determined number from partial disclosure. That reframing is what rules out macro nowcasting and prediction markets, and what makes company guidance the single most valuable object in the corpus.

Guidance is not neutral. ADI has beaten its own revenue midpoint in each of the last twelve quarters. So every forecast here has one shape:

forecast = anchor × (1 + correction)

where the anchor is something the company disclosed, and the correction is a ratio whose historical distribution we measure from the corpus. A team that parrots the guidance submits 3,900. We submit 3,965, and we can show why.

How it works

  1. 1,139 corpus documents of filings, call transcripts and slides.
  2. corpus.load() applies the as-of cutoff, the point-in-time gate.
  3. Extraction runs regexes over repeating disclosure shapes.
  4. Output is the observation table: metric, period, value, kind, as-of date and citation. It is the single source for everything downstream.
  5. Calibration pairs each guidance figure to the actual that later reported the same period, shrinks the bias and emits a sigma.
  6. Three estimators run per metric: guidance realisation, seasonal share, and a spread bridge or regression.
  7. Reconciliation combines them by inverse variance, with a disagreement gate.
  8. Validation checks units and plausibility bands, clamping rather than blanking.
  9. The workbook writer fills the exact template cells and writes a run log.
  10. The backtest replays the whole pipeline with an earlier as-of date.

Extraction is decoupled from inference

python -m forecast.build_table parses the corpus once and writes a frozen JSON table per company. Estimators read only that artefact. This keeps the expensive parse out of the inference loop and lets work on the four companies proceed independently.

Why regex and not a language model for extraction

The high-value disclosures repeat verbatim. ADI's outlook sentence has been worded identically for years:

For the third quarter of fiscal 2026, we are forecasting revenue of
$3.9 billion, +/- $100 million ... adjusted operating margin of
approximately 49.0%, +/-100 bps ... adjusted EPS to be $3.30, +/-$0.15.

One pattern yields 216 guidance rows going back to FY2023, deterministically and reproducibly. A model call would be slower, non-deterministic, and no more accurate on text this regular. Models earn their place on irregular prose, not on this.

Research and financial reasoning

The observation table is not only provenance. It is the training data for the corrections. Every estimator is an anchor plus a ratio measured from history, in one of three shapes.

The three estimator shapes and their anchors
ShapeAnchorCorrection measured from history
Guidance realisationGuidance midpointactual / guide_mid per closed quarter
Seasonal shareFull-year guideQuarter's share of the fiscal year
Spread bridgeA guided sibling metricGap in percentage points between the two

The third shape matters more than it sounds. ADI guides adjusted operating margin, but the workbook asks for adjusted gross margin. We bridge from what management discloses to what we must submit, using a spread whose stability we can measure rather than a number we invent.

Shrinkage, so a short history cannot swing a forecast

With a dozen observations the sample mean is a noisy estimate of the true bias, and over-trusting it turns a good anchor into a bad forecast. Each raw correction is pulled toward "the company's guidance is right" by an empirical-Bayes factor:

shrunk = neutral + (n / (n + 5)) × (raw − neutral)

ADI's revenue bias is +2.35% raw, shrunk to +1.66%. A metric with two observations barely moves off its anchor; one with twenty moves most of the way. The prior strength of 5 is deliberately timid, because over-correcting a good anchor is the failure mode the accuracy rubric punishes hardest.

Combining estimators by precision, not by taste

Every estimator must report a sigma derived from its own historical dispersion. They are then combined by inverse variance, w ∝ 1/σ², so a tight estimator dominates a loose one with nothing hand-tuned. An estimator that cannot state its dispersion honestly has no weight to contribute. Where the observed spread between estimators greatly exceeds their stated sigmas, the metric is flagged for human review rather than quietly averaged.

Consensus, where it actually exists

We grepped all 1,139 documents. Genuine analyst consensus appears for Hays only, because UK-listed companies publish company-compiled consensus; the hits for the other three are macroeconomic commentary and governance boilerplate. Hays' Q4 trading statement gives both the benchmark and a steer on where to sit relative to it: consensus of £43.5m for FY26 pre-exceptional operating profit against a £37.0–46.0m range, with management saying they expect to land at the top of it. There are no individual analyst estimates anywhere in the corpus.

Important design choices

We do not try to reconstruct the Wall Street benchmark

The obvious framing is "estimate what the Street thinks, then beat it". We rejected it. The score is |our miss| / max(|Street miss|, floor), and the Street number appears only in the denominator — nothing we can influence. Estimating it would add a second layer of error to a quantity that never enters our half of the fraction. We minimise our own absolute error instead, and use consensus only where disclosed, as a sanity check and as a marker of when we are taking a differentiated bet.

No fitted machine learning

Twenty to forty observations per metric, twelve metrics with no shared feature space, and no holdout to validate against. A gradient-boosted tree here would learn noise and present it confidently. We use empirical-Bayes shrinkage and inverse-variance combination instead — methods whose assumptions we can state and whose parameters we measured on held-out quarters. The one exception planned is a single-regressor OLS for Deere's Production & Precision Ag operating profit against segment sales, where operating leverage is a real and stable relationship.

Validation clamps, it does not refuse

A missing forecast scores 5.0, the worst outcome available. So implausible values are clamped to the plausibility band and a warning is recorded, rather than raising and leaving a blank cell. Only unit errors are fatal, because those are always bugs rather than judgements. An earlier draft of the schema raised on any out-of-band value; that would have been capable of destroying a metric at 17:40.

Rejected as a poor fit for this event

Prediction-market and macroeconomic priors (the target periods have already closed); peer and supplier read-through graphs (the corpus is siloed per company and the cost is high); analyst reliability modelling (no individual analyst estimates exist in the data); a UI (not in the judging rubric).

Checks and tests

33 tests run in 0.35s with no database and no network, covering fiscal-calendar anchors, point-in-time integrity, unit traps and the failure policy.

  • Point-in-time integrity. For every company, no document newer than the cutoff survives load(). This is the invariant the backtest rests on.
  • Fiscal calendars. Eight anchors taken from real reported results. All four companies run different fiscal years and two label them in opposite directions, so the first implementation put every quarter-end date in the following quarter.
  • Unit traps. A percentage outside ±100 and a Hays EPS below 1 (pounds entered where pence are required) both fail loudly.
  • Magnitude filter. Extracted values outside a plausible range are dropped and logged rather than poisoning the calibration they feed.
  • Registry agreement. Every run asserts the internal metric registry matches companies.json on labels and units before anything else happens.

Three real bugs this caught

Found by cross-checking extracted values against the Q2 FY2026 8-K by hand. The outlook regex swallowed sentence-ending full stops, so +/- $0.15. failed to parse. Positional table parsing returned the wrong column for every percentage row — adjusted gross margin of 73.0% came back as 0.00. And one document has mangled table markup that merges cells:

| Revenue | $ | 3,623 $ 2,640 | 37 % |

which caused revenue to be read as 37. None of these would have been visible without checking against the source document. It is the reason the plausibility band and magnitude filter exist.

Does the calibration actually work?

The claim is that guidance is biased and correcting it helps. That is testable, so we test it out of sample. For each closed period the calibration is refit on a corpus loaded with as_of set to the day before that guidance was published, then applied to it. Leakage would require documents to travel backwards in time.

Across 51 held-out periods the correction beat parroting the guidance in 41 of them (80%). Scored with the competition's own formula — error over benchmark error, floored and capped at 5.0, with management guidance standing in for the Wall Street benchmark we are never shown — the mean is 0.85 and the median 0.53.

One honest caveat. adj_eps has a mean score of 1.09 despite a 0.65 error ratio, because several quarters where guidance was almost exactly right hit the denominator floor. The median is the fairer summary of that metric, and both are reported here rather than only the flattering one. We also caught ourselves reporting an unfloored mean of 1.03 at first, which made a clearly good result look like no result; the ratio is unbounded above, so near-zero benchmark errors dominated it.

Reproducing the run

uv venv --python 3.12 .venv
uv pip install --python .venv/bin/python pydantic openpyxl pytest

.venv/bin/python -m pytest tests/ -q          # 33 tests
.venv/bin/python -m forecast.build_table      # observation tables + calibration
.venv/bin/python -m forecast.backtest         # walk-forward validation

The company and metric specification is read from challenge/companies.json and never hardcoded; verify_registry() fails the run if the internal registry disagrees with it on any label or unit. Point-in-time replays write to suffixed artefacts so a deliberately blinded run cannot overwrite the production table.

Known weaknesses

  • Three of four companies are not yet wired. Home Depot, Hays and Deere have no extractor at the time of writing. The framework generalises, but that is a claim until it is done.
  • Extraction is brittle by construction. Regexes tuned to a company's house style break if that style changes. We accept this because it is deterministic and auditable, and because the magnitude filter turns a silent wrong number into a logged dropped row — but a reworded outlook sentence would yield nothing at all.
  • The calibration assumes the bias persists. ADI has beaten guidance for twelve straight quarters during a semiconductor upcycle. If management resets its guidance philosophy, or the cycle turns, the correction points the wrong way. Shrinkage limits the damage; it does not prevent it.
  • Twelve observations is a small sample. The lookback window is capped at 12 quarters deliberately, since older quarters describe a different business, but that leaves the sigma estimates themselves noisy.
  • The backtest benchmark is guidance, not Wall Street. Beating management's midpoint is not the same as beating the Street, and we cannot measure the latter. It is the closest available proxy, and we label it as a proxy.
  • Fiscal quarter boundaries are approximate. Period arithmetic uses month arithmetic with a five-day drift allowance rather than exact 52/53-week period tables. It is used for ordering and never for computing a value, but an unusual 53-week year could misplace a document.
